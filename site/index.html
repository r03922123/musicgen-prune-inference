    Technical notes · Generative music · October 2026

    Eight takes, one keeper

    How a text-to-music model writes a song, how machines judge it, why picking the best of several takes is both a quality lever and a cost problem, and what the same ideas could do for music streaming beyond generation.

    A personal research project by the author of musicgen-prune-mlx · ~12 min read · Written for readers who know ML basics but not generative audio

    How to read thisNotes in the margin

    Primer notes explain an ML or audio fundamental; Worked example notes show the arithmetic behind a number. Every figure from this project is measured on the corpus and cites its results file; claims about serving systems are marked as hypotheses.

    TL;DR

    • Generative music is a sampling problem: the same prompt gives takes of very different quality, so products generate several and keep one (best-of-N). That multiplies serving cost by N.
    • Early pruning drops weak takes partway through. Cutting 8 → 4 → 2 at 10 s and 20 s generates of the audio and keeps the same final pick on of prompts (n = ).
    • The catch is the judge. Whether the pick is any better than a random take depends heavily on which model you ask, and judges barely agree on which take is best.
    • Compute saved isn't time saved: on a T4 GPU only of each generation step is GPU work.
    • The pieces (audio-text embeddings, learned critics, cascaded pruning, pairwise preference games) transfer directly to search, cold start, previews, catalogue QA and taste onboarding.

    1. Why one prompt isn't one song

    PrimerSampling, in one line

    At every step the model outputs a probability for each possible next token, and one is drawn at random. The takes here use top-k 250 (draw only from the 250 most likely tokens) at temperature 1.0. The random seed decides every draw, so a new seed means a new song.

    A text-to-music model doesn't compute the song for "lo-fi hip hop with warm piano". It defines a probability distribution over songs and draws one sample. Change the random seed and you get a different arrangement, a different melody, sometimes a different genre. Some samples are great; some drift, clip, or go silent. (One of the 400 takes in this project is almost completely silent.)

    PrimerWhy best-of-N has diminishing returns

    Intuition, not a law: if take scores were independent draws from one normal distribution, the expected best of N would grow roughly like √(2 ln N) for large N, so going from 8 to 16 takes adds far less than going from 1 to 2. Real takes aren't that tidy, but the diminishing returns show up in practice too. That's why cheaper ways to search over takes matter more than simply raising N.

    The cheapest reliable way to raise quality without retraining is the same trick used in LLM serving: best-of-N. Generate N candidates, score each with a model, keep the top one. Recent music work reports the expected reward rising steadily with N, with smaller gains per doubling (TuneJury, 2026), and the 2026 reward model used here was shown to enable this kind of top-k filtering (CMI-RewardBench). Consumer products already expose a mild form of it: Suno returns two variations per prompt and lets the user keep one.

    The problem is cost. Best-of-8 means eight full generations for one song a listener keeps. Two questions follow, and this project separates them deliberately:

    Q1 · Fidelity

    Can we stop weak takes early and still keep the same winner?

    Q2 · Value

    Is that winner actually better than a random take, according to someone other than the model that picked it?

    2. Generators: past, present, future

    PrimerNeural audio codecs

    A codec like EnCodec is an autoencoder whose bottleneck is discrete. Each frame of audio is encoded by residual vector quantization: a first codebook captures the coarse sound, each further codebook encodes what the previous ones missed. 32 kHz audio becomes 4 × 50 = 200 tokens per second, about 160× fewer numbers than the waveform.

    PrimerDelay pattern

    MusicGen predicts all 4 codebooks in one pass by shifting codebook k by k steps, so at each step the coarse token of frame t is generated alongside finer tokens of earlier frames. One model, one pass, no cascade of separate models.

    Past · 2018–2022

    Notes, then raw audio

    Early neural music models generated symbolic music (MIDI-like note events), which is compact but needs a synthesiser and can't capture timbre or production. Jukebox (2020) went straight to raw audio by compressing it into discrete codes with a VQ-VAE and modelling them with Transformers. Impressive, but rendering a minute of audio took hours.

    The model in this game · 2023

    Audio as tokens

    MusicLM (Jan 2023) and MusicGen (Jun 2023) made text-to-music practical by treating audio like language. In MusicGen, a neural codec (EnCodec) compresses 32 kHz audio into 4 parallel streams of discrete tokens at 50 frames per second. A Transformer decoder, conditioned on a T5 text embedding, predicts those tokens one frame at a time, and the codec decodes them back to sound. A 30 s clip is 1,500 decoding steps. MusicGen-small (~300M parameters) made every take you heard.

    Present · 2024–2026

    Full songs, two families

    Commercial systems now produce full songs with vocals and structure. Open models split into autoregressive token models (MusicGen's lineage) and latent diffusion, which denoises a whole clip's latent representation over many steps (Stable Audio Open, 2024). The distinction matters for pruning: an autoregressive model reveals the song left to right in time, so you can judge a 10-second prefix; a diffusion model reveals it coarse to fine, so you judge a blurry draft of the whole clip (Progressive Seed Pruning does this for images).

    PrimerClassifier-free guidance (CFG)

    To follow the prompt more closely, the model runs twice per step, with and without the text, and pushes the prediction away from the unconditioned one (scale 3.0 here). It doubles the batch: "batch 1" in the profiler is really 2 rows.

    PrimerDiffusion vs autoregressive

    Autoregressive models write tokens left to right in time. Diffusion models start from noise over the whole clip and denoise it in many steps, so an early draft covers the full 30 s at low detail.

    Where it's heading. Three directions look most consequential for anyone serving these models: (1) inference-time scaling, spending compute on smarter search over samples rather than bigger models; (2) preference fine-tuning (RLHF/DPO-style) on human choices, so a single sample is good more often and the N you need shrinks; and (3) learned early-value predictors that read a prefix, or the model's own hidden state, and estimate the final score, so pruning decisions don't depend on a general-purpose judge.

    3. Critics: how machines judge music

    PrimerContrastive embeddings (CLIP, CLAP)

    Two encoders, one for audio and one for text, are trained so matching audio–caption pairs land close together and mismatched pairs far apart. The cosine similarity between a clip and a caption then works as a zero-shot "does this match?" score.

    PrimerReward models

    A reward model is trained on human choices ("A sounds better than B") to output a score whose differences predict those choices. It's the same object used to align LLMs with RLHF, applied to music.

    Best-of-N is only as good as the scorer. Music is a hard case: "good" mixes text fidelity (did it follow the prompt?), production quality, and taste. The judges available have changed a lot in three years.

    Past

    Distribution metrics

    Fréchet Audio Distance (2018) compares the statistics of a set of generated clips with real music. Useful for comparing models, useless for picking one take.

    2022–2025

    Embeddings and aesthetics

    CLAP (2022) learns a shared audio-text embedding space, the audio counterpart of CLIP; cosine similarity to the caption is a text-match score. It's this game's AI picker. MuQ-MuLan (2025) is a newer music-specific audio-text model ("The Literalist"). Audiobox Aesthetics (Meta, 2025) predicts four perceptual axes, including content enjoyment ("The Vibe").

    2026

    Reward models from human preferences

    CMI-RM (ICML 2026) is trained on large preference datasets, part pseudo-labelled and part human-annotated, to score musicality and text alignment ("The Newcomer"). Live arenas such as Music Arena (2025) now collect pairwise human votes between models at scale.

    PrimerSpearman ρ, within vs between

    Spearman's ρ is the correlation of ranks (1 = identical order, 0 = unrelated). Between prompts compares each prompt's average score: do two judges agree which prompts went well? Within a prompt compares the order of its eight takes, which is what picking a winner needs.

    PrimerBootstrap confidence intervals

    Every CI here resamples the prompts with replacement 10,000 times and recomputes the statistic; the middle 95% of results is the interval. With only 40 prompts, intervals are wide, and they're shown on purpose.

    4. Early pruning, and the game you just played

    Worked exampleWhere 58% comes from

    Eight takes for the first 10 s, four for the next 10 s, two for the last 10 s: 8·10 + 4·10 + 2·10 = 140 take-seconds instead of 8·30 = 240. 140 / 240 ≈ 58%.

    PrimerThe KV cache

    A Transformer decoder stores the keys and values of every past token so each new step attends to them without recomputation. Each take is one row of that cache; pruning deletes rows, so every later step processes fewer of them.

    Early pruning is successive halving, the idea behind Hyperband for hyperparameter search and, more recently, Speculative Rejection for LLM best-of-N: run all candidates a little, score them, keep the best fraction, repeat. Two engineering details make it exact rather than approximate:

    • Per-candidate random noise. Each take's sampling noise is keyed by its own ID, not its position in the batch, so removing other takes never changes a survivor. The first 10 seconds of a saved 30 s take are therefore exactly what a pruned run would have produced. That's why this game can replay pruning from saved audio, and why every number below comes from an offline simulation over a fixed set of generated takes.
    • Shrinking the cache. Dropped takes are removed from the Transformer's key/value cache, so later steps really do less work.
    Worked exampleBracket vs top-k

    Top-k keeps the best half of all survivors. A bracket keeps the winner of each pair. With 8 takes there are exactly 8! / 2⁷ = 315 distinct draws, so the cost of an unlucky draw is computed exactly, not sampled.

    Your bracket is the same algorithm with you as the scorer: 10 s, 20 s, then 30 s, keeping half each round. The one difference is pairing. Pruning keeps the top half of all survivors; a bracket keeps the winner of each pair, so an unlucky draw can pit the two best takes against each other in round one.

    5. Compute is not latency

    PrimerKernel launch overhead

    The CPU tells the GPU what to do one kernel (small GPU program) at a time, and each launch costs microseconds of CPU work. When a step is ~500 tiny kernels, the GPU finishes each one and sits idle waiting for the next. A CUDA graph records the whole sequence once and replays it with a single launch.

    A profiler trace on a T4 GPU shows why pruning saved less wall-clock time than compute. One decoding step takes ms, but the GPU is busy for only ms of it. The step launches about tiny GPU kernels, and the GPU waits for the CPU to dispatch each one. Dropping takes shrinks the kernels, not their number, so at small batch sizes the saving barely shows.

    PrimerContinuous batching

    Serving systems keep a GPU batch full by inserting new requests as soon as old ones finish, instead of waiting for the whole batch. A pruned take finishing early frees a slot for someone else, which is why pruning should show up as throughput. Caveat: removing rows from a running batch (with CFG pairs and per-row KV caches) isn't standard in common serving stacks yet.

    Two consequences an engineer should take away. First, the standard fix is capturing the decode step as a CUDA graph so it's launched once instead of ~500 times; that's untested here, so any speedup is a hypothesis. Second, in a real serving system the win from pruning is probably throughput, not latency: a batch slot freed by a dropped take can go to another user's request (continuous batching), so GPU-seconds saved turn directly into cost per song. That's also a hypothesis; this project measured compute and a single GPU's step time, not a serving fleet.

    6. What this means for the AI-music business

    Unit economics. If a product shows one "best" song per request, generation cost scales with N. Pruning and better judges are the two levers: pruning lowers the cost of a given N; a better judge raises the quality you get for it. The finding that the judge matters more than the pruning schedule is, commercially, the important one: it's cheap to keep the same winner and hard to know the winner is good.

    Supply is exploding. Deezer reported in November 2025 that about 50,000 fully AI-generated tracks arrive every day, 34% of all deliveries (Deezer/Ipsos). For a streaming platform the same machinery flips around: critics that rank takes can also triage catalogue ingestion (broken audio, silence, low production quality), though quality scores are not fraud detection and shouldn't be used as such.

    PrimerLUFS

    Loudness Units relative to Full Scale measure perceived loudness, not peak level. Streaming services normalise tracks to a target loudness; every clip in this game is matched to −16 LUFS so the louder take can't win by default.

    Trust and transparency. In the same survey, 97% of 9,000 respondents couldn't tell fully AI-generated music from human music in a blind test, and 80% wanted it labelled. Platforms are responding with disclosure: Spotify adopted the DDEX standard for AI credits in 2025, avoiding a simple AI/not-AI binary (TechCrunch). Labels say that AI was used; interactive explainers like this game show how a machine chooses, which may matter as much for listener trust.

    Rights. Model licences constrain use: MusicGen's weights are CC-BY-NC (non-commercial), which is why this site is a non-commercial research demo. A product would need a licensed generator.

    7. Beyond generation: where else this applies in streaming

    None of these components is specific to generated music. Each has a direct analogue in a streaming stack:

    PrimerBradley–Terry

    A model for pairwise choices: each item has a strength s, and P(i beats j) = sᵢ / (sᵢ + sⱼ). Fit to many brackets, it turns individual "this or that" picks into a ranking, the same model behind chess-style Elo ratings and LLM arenas.

    Previews and skips

    "How early can you tell?" is the question behind skip prediction and preview clip selection: listeners decide in the first seconds. Spotify released 160M listening sessions to study exactly this (Music Streaming Sessions Dataset, 2019). An early-predictability curve for generated takes is the same analysis for a different decision.

    Cascaded ranking

    Recommenders already prune: cheap retrieval over millions of tracks, then costlier rankers over thousands, then a re-ranker over dozens. That's successive halving with growing cost per candidate. The lessons here (pruner ≠ judge, measure same-winner and regret separately) apply unchanged.

    Search and cold start

    Audio-text embeddings like CLAP and MuQ-MuLan let you search by description ("warm lo-fi piano") and tag brand-new tracks that have no listening history yet.

    Taste onboarding

    Pairwise "this or that" choices are a low-effort, reliable way to learn preferences. A bracket gets a ranking-ish signal from 7 choices instead of 28, a natural fit for onboarding or playlist tuning, modelled with Bradley–Terry or Elo.

    Catalogue QA

    Aesthetic critics and loudness analysis (every clip here is matched to −16 LUFS) catch silent, clipped or broken audio at ingestion, before a listener does.

    Engagement formats

    Data stories are proven engagement: Wrapped 2025 reached 200M engaged users in 24 hours (MBW). Games that teach how the machine works are a possible sibling format, unproven so far.

    8. Limits and what's next

    • Small samples. 40 MusicCaps prompts and 10 slow-build prompts; every number shows its n. Treat effects as directional.
    • The critics disagree and measure different things, so "better" is judge-dependent by design.
    • What you hear isn't exactly what CLAP scored. CLAP scored each decoded prefix before loudness matching; the game plays a cut of the full 30 s decode after loudness matching. The codec's context at the cut point and the level change make the two slightly different.
    • No human judgement yet. Your game choices stay in your browser; the site has no analytics, cookies or third-party requests. A future version with consented, aggregated votes would give the human early-predictability curve this project is missing.
    • Next experiments: CMI-RM as the pruner instead of CLAP; the generator's own token probabilities as a free early signal; CUDA graphs to turn saved compute into saved time.
